Before my current role in cybersecurity, I worked in content and product marketing for cloud, identity and cybersecurity companies. At LoginRadius I shaped the messaging and positioning for a customer identity platform, explaining identity, authentication and data privacy to developers and the people who buy for them.

In this essay, I describe that work first, and then what I think about marketing cybersecurity products.

## Writing about technology

Before LoginRadius, at a cloud infrastructure provider and later a digital engineering firm, I wrote about technology for enterprise audiences in India, the US, the UK and Australia: articles, case studies, landing pages, video scripts, launch announcements and API documentation. I also ghostwrote articles for executives, which were published under their names.

## Writing for what developers search for

I rebuilt the LoginRadius engineering blog's editorial strategy around what developers search for (developer search intent) and around identity education, and organic traffic doubled in six months.

We focused on SEO hygiene, and then we looked into what developers are specifically looking for that crosses our product domain. We analyzed the resources being shown to them in search, found gaps, and addressed them with higher-quality content supported by working code samples maintained on GitHub. Sometimes, a developer would want to implement authentication for their Node API: we would first explain how it works in general, and then, in the same or another blog post, how it works with our product as well.

## The guest-author program

I ran the blog as an open-source project on GitHub, with a paid guest-author program: outside developers wrote posts for it, and every post went through review by our engineers. The program produced more than thirty posts from more than twelve outside developers.

The pull requests on the [repository](https://github.com/LoginRadius/engineering-portal) are a record of that editorial work; 144 of the program's pull requests were merged.

Maintaining the blog as an open-source project felt natural when the focus is on developers and their experience. We were able to participate in Hacktoberfest, the open-source month that DigitalOcean used to conduct.[^hacktoberfest] We encouraged newcomers to contribute in any way possible, even as small as correcting spellings, so that anyone who wanted to be involved in open source did not feel daunted.

## Marketing cybersecurity products

### Pain points and technical value

When marketing cybersecurity products, we need to understand the pain points very clearly, and the solution we propose should clearly show its technical value. Testimonials are important, and so is showing how the product works and how it solves a specific pain point.

### Language, audience and the funnel

We should be very selective about the language we use, which can gain trust as well as lose it if we get it wrong. For example, are you targeting the enterprise buyer or a startup, or are you selling a product where the influencer is different from the buying decision maker? Then you have to plan whom you target at which stage of the marketing funnel, although you don't have to be too strict about the boundaries within the funnel itself.

### Interest and trust

Creating interest in your product, or gaining attention, is the easier part; building trust is key to a purchase decision, or even a proof-of-concept (PoC) decision.

Technical products are complex, and buyers are usually skeptical about unproven players promising something good. This is also a reason why bigger deal values often go to known vendors, or to vendors with whom top executives have personal relationships.

Testimonials should come from similar-looking personas and companies, so that prospective buyers feel more at home; it elevates trust in your brand or product.

### Where my experience lies

I have more experience in marketing technical products to technical buyers. Sometimes a technical product has a buyer persona in a traditional enterprise, where the marketing dynamics are different; this is not an area of strong expertise for me, but I can adapt.

AI products are as wide as software; it really depends on who your purchaser and users are.

## Close

Most of what I know about marketing technical products, I learned by writing for developers first: rebuilding a blog around what developers search for, running it as an open-source project that made contributing approachable even for a first-time newcomer, and explaining identity with working code samples. When I market cybersecurity products, the same thinking applies: understand the pain point clearly, show the product's technical value, choose the language for the audience you are actually selling to, and build trust — with testimonials from buyers who look like the ones you want, and with proof of how the product works. That trust is what turns attention into a purchase or a proof-of-concept decision.
