Before I worked in cybersecurity, I worked in content and product marketing for cloud, identity and cybersecurity companies. At LoginRadius I shaped the messaging and positioning for a customer identity platform, explaining identity, authentication and data privacy to developers and the people who buy for them.

The theme I set for this site is marketing technical products, AI, cybersecurity and developer tools among them. In this essay, I describe that work first, and then what I think about marketing cybersecurity products.

## What the work was

Before LoginRadius, at a cloud infrastructure provider and later a digital engineering firm, I wrote about technology for enterprise audiences in India, the US, the UK and Australia: articles, case studies, landing pages, video scripts, launch announcements and API documentation.

## Writing for what developers search for

I rebuilt the LoginRadius engineering blog's editorial strategy around what developers search for (developer search intent) and around identity education, and organic traffic doubled in six months.

> **Note for Raghunath:** What did you change on the blog, how did you choose topics, and how did the product come into a post that answered a developer's question?

## The guest-author program

I ran the blog as an open-source project on GitHub, with a paid guest-author program: outside developers wrote the posts, and our engineers reviewed every one of them. The program produced more than thirty posts from more than twelve outside developers.

The pull requests on the [repository](https://github.com/LoginRadius/engineering-portal) are a record of that editorial work; 144 of them were merged.

> **Note for Raghunath:** Why did you set the blog up this way, and what did it do for the blog's credibility with developers, in your view?

## Ghostwriting for executives

At those two companies, I also ghostwrote articles for executives, which were published under their names.

At LoginRadius, I turned in-house expertise on data privacy and passwordless authentication into long-form eBooks for customer education; they were published under an executive's name, so I do not link them here.

> **Note for Raghunath:** Did ghostwriting teach you anything about positioning, and if so, what?

## Marketing cybersecurity products

### Pain points and technical value

When marketing cybersecurity products, we need to understand the pain points very clearly, and the solution we propose should clearly show its technical value. Testimonials are important, and so is showing how the product works and how it solves a specific pain point.

> **Note for Raghunath:** What does "technical value" mean to you, and how do you show it?

### Language, audience and the funnel

We should be very selective about the language we use, which can gain trust as well as lose it if we get it wrong. For example, are you targeting the enterprise buyer or a startup, or are you selling a product where the influencer is different from the buying decision maker? Then you have to plan whom you target at which stage of the marketing funnel, although you don't have to be too strict about the boundaries within the funnel itself.

> **Note for Raghunath:** Is there an example from your own work (at LoginRadius or since) of a pain point you started from, or of wording that gained or lost trust with a particular audience?

### Interest and trust

Creating interest in your product, or gaining attention, is the easier part; building trust is key to a purchase decision, or even a proof-of-concept (PoC) decision.

Technical products are complex, and buyers are usually skeptical about unproven players promising something good. This is also a reason why bigger deal values often go to known vendors, or where top executives have personal relationships with the vendor.

> **Note for Raghunath:** Are testimonials how you build that trust, and what makes a testimonial useful?

### Where my experience lies

I have more experience in marketing technical products to technical buyers. Sometimes a technical product has a buyer persona in a traditional enterprise, where the marketing dynamics are different; this is not an area of strong expertise for me, but I can adapt.

> **Note for Raghunath:** Does this hold for AI products and developer tools as well, or where does it differ?
