In August 2025, I took part in Kaggle's Red-Teaming Challenge on OpenAI's open-weight gpt-oss-20b. It was a one-off. I have not done red-teaming since, the field has moved a lot, and I am not current on it. What I kept from the exercise is how I think about the model's response to a question and the conversation around it.

My goal in the writeup I submitted to the challenge was to test whether the model's safety layers could withstand indirect or nuanced prompting, using conversation-based strategies that mirror how real users might bypass safeguards, intentionally or not.

## The challenge and my writeup

gpt-oss-20b is an open-weight model, which means OpenAI released its weights for anyone to download and run.

The challenge asked participants to investigate vulnerabilities and harmful behaviors in the model and report their findings.[^challenge] I worked with two conversation-based techniques, narrative prompting and reference prompting.

My submitted writeup is [on Kaggle](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/openai-gpt-oss-20b-red-teaming/writeups/narrative-coaxing-tricks-gpt-oss-20b-to-output-dis). Here I explain the two techniques and what I think the exercise showed, without reproducing the harmful outputs.

## Narrative prompting

Narrative prompting put sensitive requests inside fictional dialogue or storytelling, where the model took on a role and completed the conversation.

With this framing, I got the model to produce disallowed information as it continued the story. I presume the narrative changed how the model responded to the request. The output does not tell me which part of its training or which internal mechanism produced that response.

## Reference prompting

Reference prompting raised sensitive topics through indirect references, such as their appearance in popular culture or medicine, before escalating toward more direct questions.

The reference gave the conversation a starting point, and each question moved closer to the material I was testing for. I presume that staying close to the previous question in wording and subject was part of why this worked, though I did not test that separately from the other differences between the prompts.

## What the exercise showed

Both techniques were conversation-based, and with them I got the model to produce disallowed information. This is what happened in the conversations I tested. To say how often they work, or how they compare with direct prompts, I would need repeated tests under the same conditions; I am not reporting that comparison here.

What I take from it now is that how a question is framed is part of what the model responds to. The same subject, asked directly or reached through a story or a reference, gives the model different context. That is the part of this one exercise I still use when explaining prompting to colleagues and thinking about it in my work.

[^challenge]: Kaggle, [Red-Teaming Challenge - OpenAI gpt-oss-20b](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/openai-gpt-oss-20b-red-teaming), hosted by OpenAI.
