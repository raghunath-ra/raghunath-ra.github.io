In August 2025, I took part in Kaggle's Red-Teaming Challenge on OpenAI's open-weight gpt-oss-20b. It was a one-off, and I have not done red-teaming since. I describe it here as an exercise that informed how I think about prompting, not as an assessment of current models or safeguards.

My goal in the writeup I submitted to the challenge was to test whether the model's safety layers could withstand indirect or nuanced prompting, using conversation-based strategies that mirror how real users might bypass safeguards, intentionally or not.

## The challenge and my writeup

gpt-oss-20b is an open-weight model, which means OpenAI released its weights for anyone to download and run.

The challenge asked participants to investigate vulnerabilities and harmful behaviors in the model and report their findings.[^challenge] My work focused on how a conversation's framing could affect refusals.

My submitted writeup is [on Kaggle](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/openai-gpt-oss-20b-red-teaming/writeups/narrative-coaxing-tricks-gpt-oss-20b-to-output-dis). This essay stays at the level of method and interpretation, rather than reproducing the harmful outputs.

## Narrative prompting

Narrative prompting put sensitive requests inside fictional dialogue or storytelling, where the model took on a role and completed the conversation.

In the conversations I tested, the model produced disallowed information while continuing the narrative. My interpretation is that the narrative context affected the response, but the output alone does not establish which training examples or internal mechanisms caused it.

## Reference prompting

Reference prompting raised sensitive topics through indirect references, such as their appearance in popular culture or medicine, before escalating toward more direct questions.

Here the initial frame was a reference rather than a fictional role. The conversation moved from that reference toward more direct requests. I suspect that this continuity mattered, although I did not isolate it from the other differences between prompts.

## What the exercise showed

Both techniques were conversation-based, and with them I got the model to produce disallowed information. That establishes that the tested conversations could get past safeguards in that setting. It does not establish a general success rate, or that these techniques outperform direct prompts across models and configurations.

To make a broader comparison, I would need repeated tests with the model version, configuration and full conversations recorded, a consistent criterion for judging outputs, and comparable direct-prompt tests. I do not report that controlled comparison here.

## What I take from it now

The useful lesson for my work is to test the surrounding conversation as well as the final question. A direct request and a request reached through a story or a reference are different test cases. That is the part of this exercise I still use when thinking about how a model responds to prompting.
