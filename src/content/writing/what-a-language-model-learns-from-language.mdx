Over the past few months, I have observed one peculiarity with AI, especially with large language models, or LLMs (as future AI systems may grow beyond LLMs): sometimes the answer refers to a meaning or a version of something that is different from what I mean when I ask. I have been thinking about this in relation to how a model learns language patterns as mathematical relationships, and reapplies what it learned when it answers.

I use "meaning drift" to describe that gap. A word's meaning can change over time, the model can be answering from old information, or I may not have explained which meaning I intend. These are different reasons for the gap, and what I give the model needs to account for the one I am dealing with.

## What the model does with your words

This is how I explain it for a text-generating transformer model. The tokenizer breaks the text into tokens, and each token becomes a vector of numbers, taken from a lookup table that the model learned in training.

The numbers go through the transformer. Attention combines information from tokens in the context, and the feed-forward layers apply further transformations using what was learned in training. Much of this work is matrix multiplication, repeated across many layers. The relationships the model learned are represented in its parameters.

At the end, the model scores possible next tokens. Softmax turns those scores into probabilities, and the decoding method determines which token is selected. It may pick the highest-scoring token, or sample using settings such as temperature, top-k and top-p; the controls available depend on the service.[^generation] Then the model does it again, with the selected token included in what it reads, until the answer is done.

All of this is mathematics applied to learned patterns. What the model gives back also depends on the context it receives when I ask, which can include information that was not in its training text.

## Language is not born out of rules

Language itself has a nuance: it comes from humans living in societies and using it with one another. The rules we write down come afterwards, derived from literature and other language artifacts, for learning and for standardization. Language continues to evolve, at a different pace at different times.

Bryan Garner's *Garner's Modern English Usage* is one reference I use when thinking about changing usage.[^garner]

## What the training data carries

During the LLM training phase, I presume, the model is trained on language patterns created at different times, with many English words taking different meanings over those times. Human societies in different geographies also interpret words, and combinations of words (phrases), in different ways.

The model can learn these different uses as relationships, though I cannot assume that it learns all of them accurately. It also answers at a later time than when its training text was collected, sometimes much later. An AI system can retrieve newer information and put it into context, but the model's trained parameters do not update just because something new has been written online.

## Meaning drift

Words themselves can take different meanings over time. The word "nice", for example, has older senses such as "foolish" or "silly", while today it commonly means "pleasant".[^nice] This is an example of how language changes; whether a model gives an older meaning back depends on the model and the context of the question.

In one example from my own use, the model was asked about Omarchy, a Linux distribution based on Arch Linux,[^omarchy] and referred to an older version instead of the latest one. Here the name had stayed the same, but the product had changed. This is old version information, rather than a word changing its meaning, although the gap I get as a user is similar: the answer is about something different from what I mean today.

I presume that how much has been written about a subject, and how recently, can be part of this. But I cannot tell from the Omarchy answer whether that was the reason. Even if Omarchy becomes more popular, the model still needs current information about the version I am asking about.

I can tell the model at the start, "When I ask something about Omarchy, always use the latest version." That explains what I mean, but I also need to give it the current documentation, or ask a system with retrieval tools to check the version and its source. The instruction alone does not give the model information it does not have.

## What I ask of an AI system

In my use case, I want agentic AI systems to communicate back, or write, in words, phrases and sentence lengths that carry concrete meaning and information to the maximum possible extent, and that stay honest to the current times at which the model or AI system is responding.

This doesn't have to mean an AI model should be restricted to using words or phrases that only have concrete meanings, with a strong assumption that most human societies understand them as concrete, without meaning drift. Language keeps changing, for the reasons above.

What I ask for is only for my use case.

I put this to AI agents in a guide that I am writing for them. The guide contains no AI-generated text; I am writing it with keyboard typing and my own thinking, mostly as a lightly constrained train of thought with minimal editorial changes. It is set up in an unusual layout size and a fixed-width font. It is my attempt to convey to AI how I want it to work, and to limit meaning drift to the extent the English language permits.

## What I can control

The part I control is what I give the model when I ask: the instructions, the context and the sources that explain what my words mean in this situation. In my own work, this is where I put my effort, through context engineering and through guides like the one I am writing for agents. I still need to check whether the answer follows what I meant and uses the information I gave it.
