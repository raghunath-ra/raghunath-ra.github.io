Over the past few months, I have observed one peculiarity with AI, especially with large language models, or LLMs (as future AI systems may grow beyond LLMs): a model relies on attributing mathematical logic and relationships to language and its intricacies, and it then reapplies this gigantic mathematical modeling when it interacts with its users, both agentic users and humans.

All of this translates to learning patterns and reapplying them, based on mathematics-powered computational logic. In this essay, I explain what that means for the words a model gives back to you: where those words come from, why their meaning can drift from what you mean today, and what I ask of an AI system because of it.

## What the model does with your words

This is how I explain it. A language model takes your text, and its tokenizer breaks the text into tokens; each model provider has its own tokenizer and vocabulary. Each token then becomes a vector of numbers, taken from a lookup table that the model learned in training.

The numbers go through the transformer. Attention works out how each token relates to the others, and the feed-forward layers are where much of what the model learned is believed to be stored. Most of this work is matrix multiplication, repeated across many layers.

At the end, the model scores every possible next token; softmax turns those scores into probabilities, and sampling picks one token, shaped by settings such as temperature, top-k and top-p. Then the model does it again, one token at a time, until the answer is done.

Every step is mathematics applied to patterns found in text. What the model has for a word is the set of relationships it learned from the text it was trained on, and it reapplies those relationships when it answers you.

## Language is not born out of rules

Language itself has a nuance: it is not born out of rules. It is born out of human evolution and humans becoming societal. The rules come afterwards; they are derived from known literature and language artifacts, for learning purposes and for standardization.

This means that language evolves continuously, and it changes direction at a different pace at different times. This is well noted by Bryan Garner's *Garner's Modern English Usage*.[^garner]

## What the training data carries

During the LLM training phase, I presume, the model is trained on language patterns created at different times, with many English words taking different meanings over those times. Human societies in different geographies also interpret words, and combinations of words (phrases), in different ways.

The model learns all of these meanings as relationships and reapplies them when it answers. It learns them only up to the point at which its training text was collected, and it answers its users at a later time, sometimes much later.

## Meaning drift

By meaning drift, I mean the gap between what a word or a name means to you at the time you ask, and what it meant in the text the model learned it from.

When you explain something to a T, the model will most likely follow what you have asked. But sometimes a user would say, "I would like to know something about Omarchy,"[^omarchy] and the model answers about an older version of Omarchy instead of the latest version. Omarchy also follows a lagged update cycle of the rolling releases of Arch,[^omarchy-updates] and the model might or might not have gotten this into its context.

Omarchy is just one example. The pattern behind it is that meaning drift happens when something is not as popular. I presume the reason is the training data: a subject that is not as popular has fewer texts written about it, and fewer recent ones, so the patterns the model learned for it are fewer and older. As the subject becomes more popular, more people write about it, newer patterns reach the training data, and the issue subsides. I expect this to happen with Omarchy itself as it becomes more popular.

<PullQuote>Meaning drift happens when something is not as popular.</PullQuote>

Things like this can be fixed with early instructions or context engineering. For example, I can tell the model at the start, "When I ask something about Omarchy, always use the latest version." That instruction puts the meaning I intend into the model's context, so the model does not depend only on the patterns it learned in training.

## Why this is not a case for restricting the model

This doesn't have to mean an AI model should be restricted to using words or phrases that only have concrete meanings, with a strong assumption that most human societies understand them as concrete, without meaning drift. Language keeps changing, for the reasons above.

What I ask for below is only for my use case.

## What I ask of an AI system

In my use case, I want agentic AI systems to communicate back, or write, in words, phrases and sentence lengths that carry concrete meaning and information to the maximum possible extent, and that stay honest to the current times at which the model or AI system is responding.

The Omarchy answer is an example of an answer that was not honest to the current times: a newer version existed when the question was asked, and the model answered from an older one.

I put this to AI agents in a guide that I am writing for them. The guide contains no AI-generated text; I am writing it with keyboard typing and my own thinking, mostly as a lightly constrained train of thought with minimal editorial changes. Because its readers are AI agents, it is set up in an unusual layout size and a fixed-width font. It is my attempt to convey to AI how I want it to work, and to limit meaning drift to the extent the English language permits.

## What I can control

The meanings a model returns come from the text it was trained on, and that text was written at different times, in different places, and in larger amounts about popular subjects than about others. That part is set when the model is trained.

The part I control is what I give the model when I ask: the instructions and the context that say what my words mean today. In my own work, this is where I put my effort, through context engineering and through guides like the one I am writing for agents.
