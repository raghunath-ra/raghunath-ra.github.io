I work in cybersecurity at a US health system, where the work involves cybersecurity engineers, network teams and caregivers. Part of my work there is making cybersecurity understandable to people who do not work in it.

To me, "understandable" means explaining why cybersecurity exists, going to a deeper level: to protect critical information from falling into the wrong hands, to prevent unauthorized access, and to ensure infosec risks are managed well. An employee may not understand all of this deeply; that should be fine. What we should focus on is what an employee needs to do, and explaining why it is necessary.

## Comic strips for caregivers

In early 2025, there was a need for comic strips on cybersecurity topics, so that busy caregivers could understand a topic by glancing at a strip for a minute or two.

The image-generation tools we had at the time were not good at placing text in images. So I created the comic panels image by image, with Microsoft 365 Copilot's built-in image generation, and then learned Figma to place the dialogue accurately on each panel.

Some of the comic strips were used in a couple of newsletters. The rest were appreciated, but they never got a chance to be used at clinical sites, because of some limitations there.

## Videos for an internal cybersecurity platform

I have also scripted and produced product videos for an internal cybersecurity platform, from storyboard to motion graphics in Premiere Pro and After Effects.

In my view, videos are necessary in a world full of LLMs. When showcasing a product, a video can show what the product does while keeping the audience engaged. An explanation from an LLM may ask more effort of the audience, which is why I see value in a video designed purposefully for the people watching it. I am talking about this use of video, rather than saying it is the right format for every task.

## Cyber awareness as a drill

What follows are my views; I have not put them into practice in my organization.

My concern is with treating cyber awareness as a drill: keep pushing training and phishing drills, and count the completions. Phishing drills are okay, but pushing the same training without considering what people already know can overburden them.

What I believe in is a careful, thoughtfully designed skills survey, and requesting training completion based on role, skill level and necessity.

There can still be awareness-month events, emails and sessions. What I want more thought put into is the structure of the program: what people already know, what their role requires, and where they need help.

If you already know what phishing is, the survey should go further. Do you understand the difference between mass phishing and spear phishing? Do you understand how MFA can reduce risks from compromised credentials, and why you still need to be careful? Do you know how to report something suspicious? Or do you shy away from reporting, thinking you may look like a fool? Do you know what the organization's policy on reporting suspicious issues or incidents is?

A person saying they know something does not tell us how they would act. I would also want to ask what they would do in a short scenario, and why, before using the answers to decide what training would help. Training required by policy or regulation would still have to be completed.

Where do the comic strips and videos fit in this? Everyone is busy with their own priorities; for them to focus on what you are asking for, you may need a more interactive approach that may blend entertainment into awareness.

The comic strips and videos are the part I have made. The survey and training by role, skill level and necessity are what I would like an awareness program to consider. In both, I want the communication to respect how busy people are, explain what they need to do, and give them a reason to pay attention.
