I work in cybersecurity at a US health system, where the work involves cybersecurity engineers, network teams and caregivers. Part of my work there is making cybersecurity understandable to people who do not work in it.

To me, "understandable" means why cybersecurity exists, going to a deeper level: to protect critical information from falling into the wrong hands, to prevent unauthorized access, and to ensure infosec risks are managed well. An employee may not understand this deeply enough; that should be fine. What you should focus on is how much an employee should do and focus on, and on explaining to them why X or Y or Z is necessary.

In this essay, I write about the comic strips and videos I have made, and then about how I think an awareness program should be designed.

## Comic strips for caregivers

In early 2025, there was a need for comic strips on cybersecurity topics, so that busy caregivers could understand a topic by glancing at a strip for a minute or two.

The image-generation tools we had at the time were not good at placing text in images. So I created the comic panels image by image, with Microsoft 365 Copilot's built-in image generation, and then learned Figma to place the dialogue accurately on each panel.

Some of the comic strips were used in a couple of newsletters. The rest were appreciated, but they never got a chance to be used at clinical sites, because of some limitations there.

## Videos for an internal cybersecurity platform

I have also scripted and produced product videos for an internal cybersecurity platform, from storyboard to motion graphics in Premiere Pro and After Effects.

In my view, videos are necessary in a world full of LLMs. Video improves interactivity or engagement while showcasing the product, where LLMs would need more effort from the audience, which may not be as persuasive as the video that we design purposefully and targetedly.

## Cyber awareness as a drill

What follows are my views; I have not put them into practice in my organization.

Most enterprises treat cyber awareness as a drill; they keep pushing training and phishing drills. Phishing drills are okay, but pushing training only overburdens people.

What I believe in is a careful, thoughtfully designed skills survey, and requesting training completion based on role, skill level and necessity.

For traditionally run awareness programs, this is much harder to do. The people with those responsibilities will keep doing what they have been doing: declaring October cyber awareness month, conducting some fun events, and pushing some emails, sessions and trainings.

I am surprised that people do not put more thought into the structural thinking I am trying to champion.

If you already know what phishing is, a survey should ask how much spam you get approximately, and how much of it you think is phishing. Do you understand the difference between mass phishing and spear phishing? Do you understand how MFA can reduce the risks from phishing or compromised credentials? Do you know how to report something if it looks suspicious? Or do you shy away from reporting things, thinking you may look like a fool? Do you know what org policy on reporting suspicious issues or incidents is? And so on.

Where do the comic strips and videos fit in this? Everyone is busy with their own priorities; for them to focus on what you are asking for, you may need a more interactive approach that may blend entertainment into awareness.

Making cybersecurity understandable is not about turning caregivers and colleagues into security experts. It is about knowing what a person needs to do, and explaining why it is necessary — whether that is a caregiver glancing at a comic strip for a minute or two, or an employee answering a skills survey that decides the training they are actually asked to complete. The comic strips and videos are the part I have made; the skills survey and training by role, skill level and necessity are the part I have not put into practice in my organization. Both rest on the same idea: respect how busy people are, and ask of them only what their role requires.
