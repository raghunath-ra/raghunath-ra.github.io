I work in cybersecurity at a US health system, where the work involves cybersecurity engineers, network teams and caregivers. Part of my work there is making cybersecurity understandable to people who do not work in it.

To me, making cybersecurity understandable starts with what a person needs to do and why it is necessary. Cybersecurity exists to protect information, prevent unauthorized access and manage infosec risks, but an employee does not need the same depth of knowledge as a cybersecurity practitioner. The explanation should connect those purposes to the decisions their role requires.

## Comic strips for caregivers

In early 2025, there was a need for comic strips on cybersecurity topics, so that busy caregivers could understand a topic by glancing at a strip for a minute or two.

The image-generation tools we had at the time were not good at placing text in images. So I created the comic panels image by image, with Microsoft 365 Copilot's built-in image generation, and then learned Figma to place the dialogue accurately on each panel.

Some of the comic strips were used in a couple of newsletters. The rest were appreciated, but they never got a chance to be used at clinical sites, because of some limitations there.

## Videos for an internal cybersecurity platform

I have also scripted and produced product videos for an internal cybersecurity platform, from storyboard to motion graphics in Premiere Pro and After Effects.

In my view, purposefully designed videos still have a place alongside LLMs. For a product demonstration, a video can show the sequence of actions and what happens on screen, rather than asking the audience to reconstruct that sequence from a written explanation. That is a reason to choose video for a particular communication task, not a claim that video is always more engaging or effective.

## Cyber awareness as a drill

What follows are my views; I have not put them into practice in my organization.

My concern with an awareness program organized around repeated training and phishing drills is that completion can become the focus, without enough attention to what each person needs to learn. Phishing drills can have a place; my objection is to requesting the same training without considering role, existing knowledge or necessity.

What I believe in is a careful, thoughtfully designed skills survey, and requesting training completion based on role, skill level and necessity.

Awareness-month events, emails and sessions can support that work, but their presence alone does not show whether people know what to do. The question I want the program to answer is which decisions employees need help making, and what training would address those gaps.

If someone already knows what phishing is, the questions can go further. Do they understand the difference between mass phishing and spear phishing? Do they know how MFA can reduce some risks from compromised credentials, and where it does not remove the need for caution? Do they know how to report something suspicious, and what the organization's policy asks them to do? Are they reluctant to report because they worry about looking foolish?

A survey can reveal perceived knowledge, confidence and barriers to reporting. It cannot establish competence just by asking people whether they understand something. A short scenario, asking what they would do and why, would give a different kind of evidence. Neither should be treated as a reason to skip training required by policy or regulation. These are parts of the approach I would want to test, not results I have already demonstrated.

Where do the comic strips and videos fit in this? Everyone is busy with their own priorities; for them to focus on what you are asking for, you may need a more interactive approach that may blend entertainment into awareness.

The comic strips and videos are work I have done; the survey-led approach is a proposal I have not put into practice. What connects them is the question of what a busy person needs to understand well enough to act. That is the question I want an awareness program to start with, and the one I would use to assess whether its communication is useful.
